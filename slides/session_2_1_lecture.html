SESSION 2.1 / FICTIONAL CLASS DATASLIDE 2 / 33
THE STRATEGY IN ONE PARAGRAPH

What is the Data Feedback Loop Strategy?

A company decides which customer signals to collect, which business decision each signal will improve, and how to keep customers' trust. Collecting more data does not make a company stronger by itself. A company gains an advantage when a small set of useful signals changes a decision, the company measures the result, and the next decision becomes better. This repeating cycle is the data feedback loop.

Use it when a business can see many customer actions but does not know which ones show a real need, or when it is tempted to collect everything "just in case."

WORDS YOU NEED FIRST

Churn rate, ARPU, and consent

Churn rateThe percentage of members who cancel in a period.
Churn rate = members who cancelled / members at the start x 100
Example: 420 / 12,000 x 100 = 3.5% per month.
ARPU
Average Revenue Per UserThe average money earned from one member in a period.
Example: StreamWise charges $5.00 per month, so ARPU is $5.00 per member per month.
ConsentA customer's clear permission to collect and use their data for a stated purpose.
Example: "We use what you finish and save to recommend shows."
SESSION 2.1 / KEY TERMS 3 OF 3SLIDE 8 / 33
THE DATA FEEDBACK LOOP

Five steps that repeat

01

Collect

Record a signal.

02

Interpret

Ask what it may mean.

03

Act

Change one action.

04

Measure

Compare one number.

05

Learn

Keep or change the action.

The loop is complete only when the result of one decision makes the next decision better.

The strategy has four rules. Each rule protects one part of this loop.

SESSION 2.1 / FEEDBACK LOOPSLIDE 9 / 33
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 1

Rule 1: Start with the decision, not the data

First name the decision you want to improve. Then collect only the signals that can change it.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

A campus cafe app wants to decide "which drink to promote on rainy days." It needs two signals: the weather and the drinks ordered that day. It does not need contact lists or browsing history.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

"Let's collect everything and find something later." Data with no decision costs money to store and adds privacy risk, but improves nothing.

SESSION 2.1 / RULE 1 OF 4SLIDE 10 / 33
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 2

Rule 2: Use strong signals; check weak signals before you act

Prefer actions that show effort or repeat behavior: finishing, saving, buying again, searching for a specific item.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

At StreamWise, 48,000 plays were started but only 9,600 were finished: 9,600 / 48,000 x 100 = 20%. A play started is a weak signal of interest; a play finished is stronger.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Treating a weak signal as proof. A viewer who stops after 10 seconds may dislike the show, or may have received a phone call.

SESSION 2.1 / RULE 2 OF 4SLIDE 11 / 33
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 3

Rule 3: Close the loop and measure one result

Collect, interpret, change one action, measure the result, and learn for the next decision.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

Students who finish a cooking show often save other cooking shows. StreamWise adds a "More easy cooking" row for them and measures their churn for one month. If churn falls from 3.5% to 2.5%, the row is kept; if not, it is changed.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Changing something but never measuring the result. Without the measure step, the company cannot learn whether the data helped.

SESSION 2.1 / RULE 3 OF 4SLIDE 12 / 33
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 4

Rule 4: Earn trust: ask permission, collect only what you need, give value back

Customers share data more willingly when they understand the purpose and receive something useful in return.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

A sign-up screen says: "We use what you finish and save to recommend shows. We never sell your viewing history." Members can turn this off at any time.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Collecting or selling data in ways customers would not expect. Even accurate data can damage the brand if customers feel watched.

SESSION 2.1 / RULE 4 OF 4SLIDE 13 / 33
GOOD EXAMPLE 1

Netflix recommendations

What happened

Netflix uses what members watch, finish, and search for to decide which titles and which artwork to show each member. It tests a change on a group of members and keeps it only if viewing improves. Netflix researchers reported that recommendations influence about 80% of the hours members stream (Gomez-Uribe and Hunt, 2015).

Why it is good

  • It starts with a clear decision: which titles to show on each home screen. (Rule 1)
  • It relies on strong signals such as finished titles, not only clicks. (Rule 2)
  • It tests each change and measures the result before keeping it. (Rule 3)
SESSION 2.1 / GOOD EXAMPLE 1SLIDE 14 / 33
GOOD EXAMPLE 2

Spotify Discover Weekly

What happened

Since 2015, Spotify has given each listener a new personalized playlist every Monday. It is built from songs the listener plays, saves, adds to playlists, and skips.

Why it is good

  • Saves and repeated plays are strong signals; quick skips show what to avoid. (Rule 2)
  • The listener receives clear value from the data: new music chosen for them. (Rule 4)
  • Each week, the listener's reaction becomes new data for the next playlist. (Rule 3)
SESSION 2.1 / GOOD EXAMPLE 2SLIDE 15 / 33
BAD EXAMPLE 1

Google Flu Trends

What happened

From 2008, Google estimated how many people had the flu by counting flu-related searches. In February 2013, it estimated more than twice the share of flu doctor visits that the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) later reported (Lazer and others, 2014). Google stopped publishing the estimates in 2015.

Why it is bad

  • A search is a weak signal: people search because of news or curiosity, not only because they are sick. (Rule 2)
  • The model was not regularly checked against real doctor-visit data and corrected. (Rule 3)
  • Big data volume created confidence without accuracy. Many signals are not the same as useful signals.
SESSION 2.1 / BAD EXAMPLE 1SLIDE 16 / 33
BAD EXAMPLE 2

Target's pregnancy prediction

What happened

The New York Times reported in 2012 that Target used shopping signals, such as unscented lotion and certain vitamins, to predict which customers were pregnant and mailed them baby-product coupons. A father complained after his teenage daughter received these coupons.

Why it is bad

  • The signals were strong and the prediction was often accurate, but customers did not expect a store to know this. (Rule 4)
  • Customers had not agreed to have sensitive facts guessed from their purchases: no clear consent.
  • Lesson: accurate data can still damage trust and the brand.

SESSION 2.1 / EXAMPLE SUMMARYSLIDE 18 / 33
BEFORE THE EXERCISES / TOOL 1

Tool 1: Test group and comparison group

Try a change on one group, not on a similar group, and compare the same number.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example: a cafe coupon

  • 500 members get a coupon (test group). 500 members get none (comparison group).
  • Coupon group: 75 order this week, or 15 of every 100.
  • Other group: 50 order, or 10 of every 100.
  • The coupon added about 5 orders for every 100 members.
USED IN EXERCISE 2

Why it matters

Without the comparison group, you cannot tell whether orders rose because of the coupon or because of something else, such as rainy weather.

SESSION 2.1 / TOOL 1 OF 3SLIDE 19 / 33
BEFORE THE EXERCISES / TOOL 2

Tool 2: From churn to money

Lower churn means more members stay and keep paying.

USED IN EXERCISE 3

Two formulas

  • Members kept per month = members x (old churn rate - new churn rate)
  • Revenue kept per month = members kept x monthly price
WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example: a cafe subscription

  • 1,000 members pay $10.00 a month.
  • Churn falls from 6% to 4%: 1,000 x 2% = 20 more members stay.
  • Revenue kept: 20 x $10.00 = $200.00 that month.
  • The gain grows every month, because those members keep paying.
SESSION 2.1 / TOOL 2 OF 3SLIDE 20 / 33
BEFORE THE EXERCISES / TOOL 3

Tool 3: Trade-off and decision rule

MEMO BULLET 2

Trade-off

What you give up to get something you value more.

Example: a fitness app refuses $2.00 per user from an advertiser (gives up money) to protect users' trust and its subscriptions (gains).

MEMO BULLET 3

Decision rule

A promise, written before you start, about which result would make you change your decision.

Example: "If churn does not fall below 4% in 90 days, we will try a different plan."

The result, not opinion, decides.

Churn rate = 160 / 2,000 x 100 = 8%

A GOOD LOOP
  • Collect: records "booked but did not attend."
  • Interpret: gives two meanings, for example the class time is inconvenient, or the member forgot.
  • Act: tests one change on a test group and compares it with a comparison group, for example a reminder for half of these members.
  • Measure: one number with a target, for example churn from 8% to 6%.
  • Learn: says what happens if the target is or is not reached.

Option A: Sell viewer profiles

EXERCISE 3 / WORKED EXAMPLESLIDE 31 / 33
EXERCISE 3 / CHECK YOUR ANSWER

Selling the data costs more than it earns

The numbers

  • Option A brings $12,000 per month.
  • If 3,600 members (30%) cancel, StreamWise loses 3,600 x $6.00 = $21,600 per month.
  • Option B keeps 12,000 x 1% = 120 members, or $600 per month, and this grows every month churn stays lower.

A strong memo

  • Usually chooses Option B, or a version of it.
  • Uses at least one number from Step 1.
  • Names a real risk: members may refuse permission, or recommendations may not lower churn.
  • States a clear rule for changing the decision.
  • Option A can still be strong if it explains real consent and gives a decision rule.
