THE SKILL IN ONE PARAGRAPH

How do you choose the right strategy?

Diagnose the business problem first, then use the Module 2 strategy that fits it. Check the evidence for that strategy's key condition, compare the options with the same numbers, and start with one strategy while planning the next.

Use this skill whenever a business has several possible plans and limited time and money.

WORDS YOU NEED FIRST

Diagnosis and condition

DiagnosisNaming the real problem before choosing a solution.
Example: "Students stop ordering after two weeks" is a diagnosis; "we need more ads" is a solution.
ConditionSomething that must be true for a strategy to work.
Example: up-selling needs a higher tier that customers value.
SESSION 2.4 / KEY TERMS 1 OF 2SLIDE 5 / 29
WORDS YOU NEED FIRST

Time to result and sequence

Time to resultHow long a plan takes before it changes the numbers.
Example: a data feedback loop may need three months of signals.
SequenceThe order in which a business uses strategies.
Example: first up-sell to cover costs, then a data loop to learn which premium items students want.
SESSION 2.4 / KEY TERMS 2 OF 2SLIDE 6 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 1

Rule 1: Name the problem before you choose a strategy

Write the problem in one sentence that does not include a solution.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

"Students order a box for two weeks and then stop" points to a customer-knowledge problem (Session 2.1), not a pricing problem.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Starting with a favorite strategy ("let's add a premium box") before knowing what is wrong.

SESSION 2.4 / RULE 1 OF 4SLIDE 7 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 2

Rule 2: Check the strategy's conditions with evidence

Each strategy works only when its key condition is true.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

Asymmetric pricing needs a side that is not price sensitive. If most farms would leave over a $200 fee, the condition is weak.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Using a strategy's words without its conditions, such as calling a business a "platform" when it does not connect two groups.

SESSION 2.4 / RULE 2 OF 4SLIDE 8 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 3

Rule 3: Compare the options with the same numbers

For each option: the change in the monthly result, the time to result, and the main risk.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

One plan adds $1,800 a month but needs three months; another adds $3,240 a month and can start next week.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Comparing one option's best case with another option's worst case.

SESSION 2.4 / RULE 3 OF 4SLIDE 9 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 4

Rule 4: Start with one strategy and plan the next

Fix the most urgent problem first, then plan which strategy comes next and why.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

First up-sell and cross-sell to stop the monthly loss, then use a data feedback loop to learn which premium items students want.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Trying all three strategies at once, so the team cannot tell which one worked.

SESSION 2.4 / RULE 4 OF 4SLIDE 10 / 29
GOOD EXAMPLE 1

Spotify: three strategies that fit together

What happened

Spotify keeps a free tier paid for by advertisers, uses listening signals to build personal playlists, and up-sells free listeners to Premium, Duo, and Family plans.

Why it is good

  • Each strategy fits a clear problem: who pays, what listeners want, and how to earn more per listener. (Rule 1)
  • The free tier creates the audience and data the other strategies need. (Rule 4)
  • Premium gives clear extra value: no ads and offline listening. (Rule 2)
SESSION 2.4 / GOOD EXAMPLE 1SLIDE 11 / 29
GOOD EXAMPLE 2

LinkedIn

What happened

Members create profiles for free. Recruiters and companies pay to search and contact members, and some members pay for Premium features.

Why it is good

  • Members are the subsidy side recruiters come for, so they stay free. (Rule 2)
  • Recruiters gain the most from the match, so they are the main money side.
  • Premium up-sells members who need more, such as job seekers, without charging all members. (Rule 1)
SESSION 2.4 / GOOD EXAMPLE 2SLIDE 12 / 29
BAD EXAMPLE 1

WeWork's 2019 IPO

What happened

WeWork rented office space on long leases and rented desks to members on short contracts. Before its planned 2019 stock listing (IPO), it called itself a technology platform and was valued at about $47 billion by private investors. When its filings were published, investors questioned that description, and WeWork withdrew the IPO in September 2019.

Why it is bad

  • It used platform words but did not mainly connect two groups who need each other. (Rule 2)
  • The numbers showed a property-rental business with large losses. (Rule 3)
  • Investors who checked the evidence lost trust, and its value fell sharply.
SESSION 2.4 / BAD EXAMPLE 1SLIDE 13 / 29
BAD EXAMPLE 2

MoviePass

What happened

In 2017, MoviePass cut its price to $9.95 a month for up to one movie a day, while paying theaters close to the full ticket price for each movie. Its managers hoped to earn money later from subscriber data. Losses grew with every movie watched, and the service shut down in 2019.

Why it is bad

  • A huge subsidy with no money side to cover it (Session 2.2, Rule 4).
  • The data plan was only a hope, not a proven feedback loop. (Rule 2)
  • One ticket could cost more than a whole month's fee. (Rule 3)

EXERCISE 2 / CHECK YOUR ANSWER

Repeat rate is 30%: use the Data Feedback Loop

  • Repeat booking rate = 300 / 1,000 x 100 = 30%.
  • PetPal does not know why owners leave (Tool 1, question 1). Pricing is not the main problem: sitters are happy and waiting.
  • Collect strong signals with permission: owner ratings, photos the sitter sent, whether the owner booked again.
  • Test one change on a test group, such as a photo update during each visit.
  • Measure the repeat booking rate, with a target such as 30% to 40%.

CampusPrint: data loop or binding cross-sell?

To: Ben Cho, CEO, CampusPrint 1. Decision and evidence: We should start with the binding cross-sell because it fits a clear need before exams (Rule 1) and 30% of 2,000 orders x $1.00 = $600 a month covers our $500 loss. Next, we will use a data feedback loop to learn which students print most. 2. Biggest risk and trade-off: The weakest point is that binding sales may drop after exams. We give up a slower, deeper data plan for now to stop the monthly loss quickly. 3. Next step and decision rule: In the next 60 days we will offer binding at checkout to half of our users and measure the attach rate. We will change this decision if it is below 20%.
