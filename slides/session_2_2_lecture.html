SESSION 2.2 / FICTIONAL CLASS DATASLIDE 2 / 29
THE STRATEGY IN ONE PARAGRAPH

What is the Asymmetric Pricing Strategy?

A platform connects two groups who need each other. With asymmetric pricing, it charges them different prices. The subsidy side pays little or nothing, because its presence attracts the other group. The money side pays, because meeting the first group is worth money to it.

Use it when a business connects two groups, one group is easily driven away by a price, and the other group would gladly pay to reach it.
SESSION 2.2 / STRATEGY OVERVIEWSLIDE 3 / 29
WORDS YOU NEED FIRST

Platform, subsidy side, money side

Two-sided platformA business that connects two groups of users who need each other.
Example: CampusJobs connects students and shops.
Subsidy sideThe side that pays little or nothing because the platform needs many of them.
Example: students use CampusJobs for free.
Money sideThe side that pays because reaching the other side is worth money to them.
Example: a shop pays to post a job and find workers.
SESSION 2.2 / KEY TERMS 1 OF 2SLIDE 4 / 29
WORDS YOU NEED FIRST

Price sensitivity, subsidy, cross-subsidy

Price sensitivityHow much a group's use drops when the price rises.
Example: if a $2.00 fee makes 60 of every 100 students leave, students are very price sensitive.
SubsidyValue the platform gives away to attract a side.
Example: free use, or a $5.00 sign-up bonus.
Cross-subsidyUsing money from one side to pay for the free service given to the other side.
Example: shop fees pay the costs of serving students for free.
SESSION 2.2 / KEY TERMS 2 OF 2SLIDE 5 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 1

Rule 1: Subsidize the side that the other side comes for

Ask: "Which group does the other group want to reach?" Keep that group free or cheap.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

Shops join CampusJobs because 6,000 students are there. Without students, no shop would post a job, so students should stay free.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Charging the side that attracts everyone else. When it leaves, the other side leaves too.

SESSION 2.2 / RULE 1 OF 4SLIDE 6 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 2

Rule 2: Charge the side that gains the most from the match

The money side usually earns money or saves time because of the other side.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

A shop that fills a job quickly saves days of searching and keeps its store running. That is worth more to a shop than a job search is worth to a student in cash.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Charging both sides the same price because it looks fair. Equal prices ignore that the two sides value the platform very differently.

SESSION 2.2 / RULE 2 OF 4SLIDE 7 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 3

Rule 3: Check price sensitivity before you set a price

Find out which side leaves when the price rises, and keep the price low for that side.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

A survey finds that a $2.00 fee would make 60% of students leave, but shops would pay $25.00 per job post if each post gets at least 20 applications.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Guessing. A price that looks small to the business ($2.00) can be large for a student budget.

SESSION 2.2 / RULE 3 OF 4SLIDE 8 / 29
THE FOUR RULES / RULE 4

Rule 4: Make the money side cover the subsidy, and protect the free side

The money side must pay enough to cover the cost of serving both sides, and must not harm the free side.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example

CampusJobs checks that job-post fees are more than its $4,000 monthly costs, and removes fake or unpaid "job" posts so students keep trusting the app.

WHAT TO AVOID

Common mistake

Giving away free service with no plan for who will pay, or letting the paying side flood the free side with low-quality offers.

SESSION 2.2 / RULE 4 OF 4SLIDE 9 / 29
GOOD EXAMPLE 1

Adobe Acrobat Reader

What happened

In the 1990s, Adobe gave away the Reader program for opening PDF files for free, and sold the Acrobat software that creates and edits PDF files to businesses.

Why it is good

  • Millions of free readers made PDF the format businesses wanted to use. (Rule 1)
  • Businesses that create documents gained the most, so they were charged. (Rule 2)
  • Readers would not have paid just to open a file, so Reader stayed free. (Rule 3)
SESSION 2.2 / GOOD EXAMPLE 1SLIDE 10 / 29
GOOD EXAMPLE 2

Google Search

What happened

People search on Google for free. Advertisers pay Google to show labeled ads next to search results.

Why it is good

  • Advertisers come because billions of people search there: searchers are the subsidy side. (Rule 1)
  • Advertisers earn money from the customers they reach: they are the money side. (Rule 2)
  • Ads pay for the free service, and labels keep searchers' trust. (Rule 4)
SESSION 2.2 / GOOD EXAMPLE 2SLIDE 11 / 29
BAD EXAMPLE 1

Unity's 2023 Runtime Fee

What happened

Unity sells tools that game developers use to build games. In September 2023, it announced a fee charged to developers each time a game was installed, including some games already released. Developers protested, Unity changed the plan within weeks, and its CEO left the following month.

Why it is bad

  • Developers bring the games that make Unity valuable; a sudden fee pushed them away. (Rule 1)
  • Unity did not first check how developers would react to a fee on installs they could not control. (Rule 3)
  • Changing prices for games already released broke developers' trust.
SESSION 2.2 / BAD EXAMPLE 1SLIDE 12 / 29
BAD EXAMPLE 2

Groupon and local merchants

What happened

Groupon sold deep-discount deals, often 50% off, from local businesses to its many shoppers, and took a share of the deal price. Many small businesses found that a deal cost them more than it earned, because most deal buyers did not return at full price.

Why it is bad

  • The money side paid for a large subsidy but did not gain enough from the match. (Rule 2)
  • Shoppers came for the discount, not for the merchant. (Rule 4)
  • Merchants who lost money did not run a second deal, so the platform lost its supply.

BEFORE THE EXERCISES / TOOL 1

Tool 1: Revenue from each side

Calculate revenue for each side, then add them.

USED IN EXERCISES 2 AND 3

Formula

Revenue = (users on side A x price for A) + (users on side B x price for B)

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example: a campus events app

  • 2,000 students pay $0.
  • 40 clubs pay $10.00 a month.
  • 2,000 x $0 + 40 x $10.00 = $400 a month.
SESSION 2.2 / TOOL 1 OF 3SLIDE 15 / 29
BEFORE THE EXERCISES / TOOL 2

Tool 2: Value per match for the money side

The money side pays only if it gets enough matches.

USED IN EXERCISE 3

Formula

Matches per post = total matches / number of posts

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Example: a tutoring app

  • 800 student requests for 100 tutor listings.
  • 800 / 100 = 8 requests per listing.
  • Tutors pay only with at least 10 requests: 8 is not enough yet.

Can the money side cover the subsidy? GameNight

Scenario

GameNight lists board-game nights at campus cafes. Players use it free and get a $2.00 snack voucher per event; GameNight pays for it.

Cafes pay $30.00 per event. This month: 40 events, 15 players each.

Your steps

  • Step 1 (Tool 1): Cafe revenue = 40 x $30.00 = $____. Voucher cost = 40 x 15 x $2.00 = $____. Profit = $____.
  • Step 2: Change one price or subsidy so the money side covers the subsidy. Explain why players still come.

TEMPLATE

EXERCISE 2 / CHECK YOUR ANSWER

Revenue $1,200 minus vouchers $1,200 = $0

  • Cafe revenue = 40 x $30.00 = $1,200. Voucher cost = 40 x 15 x $2.00 = $1,200. Profit = $0.
  • Keep the app free for players (Rule 1).
  • Raise the cafe fee a little: 40 x $40.00 = $1,600, so $1,600 - $1,200 = $400.
  • Or lower the voucher to $1.00: 40 x 15 x $1.00 = $600, so $1,200 - $600 = $600.
  • Players still come for the cafes and the games, not mainly for the voucher.

EXERCISE 3 / WORKED EXAMPLESLIDE 27 / 29
EXERCISE 3 / CHECK YOUR ANSWER

Charging everyone earns less and loses the shops

The numbers

  • Option A: 2,400 students stay; 2,400 x $2.00 + 120 x $2.00 = $5,040.
  • But applications fall to 3,600, or 12 per post, below the 20 shops need: shops are likely to leave next.
  • Option B: 300 x $25.00 = $7,500, above $4,000 costs, with 9,000 / 300 = 30 applications per post.

A strong memo

  • Chooses Option B.
  • Uses at least one number from Step 1.
  • Names a real risk: shops may post fewer jobs once they pay, or the survey may be wrong.
  • States a decision rule, e.g. "If applications per post fall below 20, we will lower the fee."
